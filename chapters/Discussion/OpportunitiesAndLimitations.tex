\section{Opportunities and Limitations of AI Agents in Software Development}

Across both parts, the project shows that AI coding agents can do substantial, genuinely useful work. They produced complete features spanning backend and frontend, generated systematic test coverage for non-trivial scenarios, and --- once given a fixed, narrow checklist instead of an open-ended instruction to "review the code" --- reliably found problems a human reviewing informally had missed, such as untested code paths or inconsistent naming. This points to a real opportunity: an agent cannot replace the judgment a human reviewer brings, but a large share of the mechanical, checklist-style part of quality review can be handed to an agent built specifically for that job, leaving human attention for the judgment calls a checklist cannot make on its own.

Part 1 had almost no structure in place, so it is tempting to assume that the moments where a human had to step in and exercise judgment were simply a consequence of that missing structure --- and that adding a Charter and a review process, as Part 2 did, would make that need for judgment go away. The project's evidence says otherwise. Even with the Charter and the mandatory Design Review in place, a human was still needed to approve or reject a plan's reasoning, or to decide what a vague ownership boundary should resolve to. That need for judgment did not disappear under a more structured process; it simply moved earlier and became concentrated at fewer decision points, mainly at plan approval, instead of being scattered throughout implementation the way it was in Part 1. So the claim this supports is a narrower one: structure changes when and how human judgment gets used in an AI-assisted workflow, not that it removes the need for that judgment.

A second limitation concerns where the Charter's rules actually came from. Several of its concrete rules --- the non-nullable foreign key requirement, the service-function error pattern, the component size limit --- trace directly back to specific mistakes already made in Part 1. This matters for how far Part 2's success can be generalized: the structured approach worked well partly because its rules were written with firsthand knowledge of exactly what had gone wrong in this codebase, using Part 1 as the source of the concrete failure modes each rule was designed to prevent.
